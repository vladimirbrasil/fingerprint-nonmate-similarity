# Literature Review: Fingerprint Identification and the Living-to-Deceased Question

This technical note reviews the literature pertaining to the question: *has a living person's fingerprint ever matched a deceased person's record, and could available databases be searched for such a case?* It surveys known identification errors, error rate studies, models of individuality, identical twin divergence, database operations, and evidentiary requirements.

## 1. Published Cases of Fingerprint Identification Error

The most prominent published cases of fingerprint identification error typically involve "close non-matches" exacerbated by cognitive bias, rather than exact duplicates. In 2004, the FBI erroneously identified Brandon Mayfield's fingerprint on a bag of detonators connected to the Madrid train bombings (Office of the Inspector General, 2006). This false positive resulted from an overreliance on a partial, distorted latent print, the presentation of a highly similar non-match by the automated database (AFIS), and subsequent confirmation bias among verifying examiners. Similarly, in 1997, Scottish police officer Shirley McKie was falsely accused of leaving her fingerprint at a murder scene (Campbell, 2011). A judicial inquiry found that the misidentification stemmed from human error when examining a marginal quality latent mark, compounding assumptions, and a lack of objective standards for complex comparisons.

## 2. Error Rate Studies

Large-scale empirical studies have quantified the foundational validity and error rates of latent print examination. Ulery et al. (2011) conducted a landmark "black-box" study of 169 latent print examiners, reporting an overall false positive rate of approximately 0.1% and a false negative rate of 7.5%. A follow-up study on repeatability and reproducibility demonstrated that examiners do not always agree with their own prior decisions on marginal prints, highlighting the subjective nature of the ACE-V (Analysis, Comparison, Evaluation, and Verification) process (Ulery et al., 2012). The President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST) cited these studies to conclude that while fingerprint analysis possesses foundational validity, it inherently carries a non-zero error rate (PCAST, 2016). The Miami-Dade Police Department study reported a much higher false positive rate, about 3%, and PCAST used its upper bound to suggest jurors be told false positives may occur in as many as 1 in 18 cases (Pacheco et al., 2014; PCAST, 2016). How to read that estimate is disputed (Ausdemore et al., 2019); the gap between ~0.1% and ~3% is itself a measure of how much depends on study design.

## 3. Models of Individuality

Theoretical models of fingerprint individuality attempt to quantify the probability of two distinct fingers sharing the same friction ridge pattern. Early efforts date back to Francis Galton, who estimated the probability of a specific fingerprint pattern to be astronomically small (Galton, 1892). A more contemporary and widely cited mathematical model by Pankanti, Prabhakar, and Jain (2002) estimated that a fingerprint with 36 minutiae shares 12 minutiae with an arbitrarily chosen fingerprint of 36 minutiae with probability 6.10 × 10⁻⁸ — far larger than the figures implied by earlier models, and computed for good-quality full prints, not latent marks. However, these theoretical probabilities assess the uniqueness of complete, perfect patterns under idealized assumptions. Critics such as Zabell (2005) argue that such models rely on oversimplified assumptions of minutiae independence and fail to account for the spatial dependencies and distortions encountered in actual forensic practice.

## 4. Identical Twins and Ridge Formation

While identical twins share identical DNA, their fingerprints are phenotypically distinct and can be differentiated by automated fingerprint identification systems (Jain, Prabhakar & Pankanti, 2002). This divergence originates during embryogenesis, where friction ridges form between weeks 10 and 16 of gestation due to localized mechanical stresses. Kücken and Newell (2005) proposed a mechanical buckling model, explaining that the buckling instability of the epidermal basal cell layer—driven by compressive stress—forms the ridge patterns. The specific geometry of these stresses is highly sensitive to the stochastic size, shape, and regression of the embryonic volar pads (Wertheim & Maceo, 2002). This inherent developmental randomness ensures that even monozygotic twins possess distinct fingerprint topographies.

## 5. Deceased Data and Database Deduplication

Operationally, large biometric databases employ routine deduplication processes to prevent a single individual from holding multiple identities. Systems like India's Aadhaar (UIDAI) and the FBI's Next Generation Identification (NGI) constantly cross-reference new enrolments against existing records. Databases containing fingerprints of the deceased, such as NamUs and Interpol's Disaster Victim Identification (DVI) records, are highly restricted and used primarily for identifying unknown remains. We found no published account of how a hit between a living person and a deceased person's record is handled. The following is our inference, not a documented procedure: such a hit would surface as a deduplication alert, and the investigation would look first for the ordinary explanations — a misfiled record, an identity fraud, a declaration of death that was wrong. In almost every case one of those would be correct, and the case would be closed on that basis, leaving no trace in the literature.

## 6. What Would Count as Evidence

Establishing a genuine case of two different individuals sharing a fingerprint requires ruling out clerical errors and record duplications, which are the most parsimonious explanations for high-scoring non-mates. In this repository (REPORT.md, Results 2 and 5), every high-scoring "different-finger" pair in the naive run turned out to be the same finger captured on another sensor, and the closest remaining pair is under examination as a probable unlinked duplicate across two collections. Duplicates also hide in the other direction: some known same-finger pairs score like strangers. Therefore, a credible claim of a living-to-deceased match would demand an unbroken chain of custody for both captures. It would also require cross-algorithmic validation and manual ACE-V examination by independent experts to confirm that the prints originate from distinct biological sources rather than database duplication artifacts.
